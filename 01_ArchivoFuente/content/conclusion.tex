\chapter*{Conclusion}

The development of a hybrid propulsion and retrofit system considers multiple variables, not only the load profile, but also the physical and economic analysis of the battery bank as a key element.

One of the main features that help make this possible is knowledge of the vessel's load profile, which allows the total inefficiency of the propulsion system to be estimated and a battery bank to be proposed that is suitable for the requirements of the operational profile.

With this in mind, it is necessary to delve into technical regulatory aspects that provide more details on the implementation of the battery bank and electrical sizing. Based on this, the main technical characteristics are provided to determine the physical size and weight of the bank, which is key information for vessels such as tugboats in the case of a retrofit.

In line with this, it is necessary to understand economic aspects that allow for analysis of the economic benefits of this project for different types of battery banks, with the aim of analyzing the economic potential of this project from an early stage and thus contributing to international greenhouse gas emissions policies.

This stage is key because it involves an assessment of implementation costs, which will largely serve to convince different investors to make a change like this. In addition, not all vessels may need the same retrofit, as depending on their useful life, they may require different levels of hybridization to achieve a return on investment. To this end, it is necessary to once again consider economic and mechanical aspects, which are complementary to the electrical technical aspects.

Finally, once the project has been deemed technically and economically viable, it is possible to obtain a general understanding of the electrical performance of the battery bank and its complementary elements, such as the power converters that regulate the DC bus. The aim is to ensure that the key element of the hybrid propulsion system will be able to meet propulsion demands and will have sufficient capacity to perform throughout a working day.

This completes the first hybridization cycle, which will subsequently lead to testing different power converters implemented with the battery bank and analyzing the electrical performance of other elements within the hybrid power system. This lays the foundations for a methodology that seeks retrofitting and hybridization, taking into account as many key elements as possible to ensure a successful process.

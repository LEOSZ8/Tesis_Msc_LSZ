\chapter{Hybrid Tugboats: Requirements and Performance}

This chapter presents the fundamental concepts associated with tugboat hybridization, its most common technological configurations, and the operational benefits associated with the incorporation of battery banks. The objective is to establish the theoretical and technical basis for the design process carried out in subsequent chapters.

Given the intermittent nature of the load profile and the dependence on diesel engines, which are oversized for low-load tasks, the implementation of the hybrid energy storage system proposed in this thesis is analyzed.

\section{General Power Sources and Energy Storage System}

In general, the hybrid tugboat relies on a triad of power sources:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Main Propulsion Engines,
	\item Auxiliary Generators,
	\item Battery Energy Storage System (BESS).
\end{itemize}

The functions of these components have changed significantly compared to conventional designs, and the aim is to contribute to both propulsion and hotel loads.

\subsection{Main Propulsion Diesel Engines}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{images/chapter03/MainPropDieselEngine2.pdf}
    \caption{Main diesel engine used in hybrid vessels.}
    \label{fig:MainDieselengine2}
\end{figure}
In hybrid vessels, the main engines are only started when high power is actually needed, as the electric motor handles low-speed maneuvers and idling. This reduces their operating hours, which increases their operational efficiency and reduces their emissions \cite{MAN} \cite{Jayaram}.

As shown in Figure \ref{fig:MainDieselengine2}, The mechanical connection via a reduction gearbox allows an electric motor to be coupled \cite{Bas} \cite{MAN}, enabling the main diesel engine to transmit its full rated power to the shaft, complementing the work of the electric motor. \cite{Jayaram}.

\subsection{Auxiliary Diesel Generators}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{images/chapter03/AuxGenSet.pdf}
    \caption{Main generator inside a hybrid vessel.}
    \label{fig:AuxGenSet}
\end{figure}

In a conventional tugboat, auxiliary generators (generator sets) power the ship's service loads \cite{SKSul}. In a hybrid tugboat, they can play a role in both powering the hotel loads and taking over propulsion. 

They are usually arranged in banks to provide different service powers (for example, four small generators instead of two large main generators) \cite{Zhang} \cite{Chua}, which makes it possible to use only one generator for low energy demand and the rest for higher demands.

As shown in Figure \ref{fig:AuxGenSet}, auxiliary diesel generators can be used to \cite{Hofman}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Deliver power to the propellers via a series connection when less power than the rated power of the main diesel engines is required.
	\item Without connecting to the propellers, it allows the batteries to be charged when there is no possibility of charging them in port. 
	\item Power supply to the tugboat hotel loads.
\end{itemize}
 
\subsection{Battery Bank}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.3\linewidth]{images/chapter03/BattBank.jpg}
    \caption{Battery bank made with multiple cells.}
    \label{fig:BattBank_scheme}
\end{figure}

The function of the BESS shown in the Figura \ref{fig:BattBank_scheme} is not only to store energy; it also complements the operation of the main diesel engines. As mentioned in section \ref{sec:Roles}, these are some of the complementary functions that the BESS can provide in a hybrid tugboat.

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Peak Shaving:} The use of a BESS solves the inefficiencies of diesel engines when faced with sudden changes in power. Instead of forcing the engine to accelerate rapidly—which causes thermal stress and polluting emissions (black smoke)—the battery supplies energy instantly. 
	\item \textbf{Spinning Reverse:} If the active diesel generator fails, the BESS instantly takes over the load, preventing a blackout and saving fuel and operating hours for the main diesel engine.
	\item \textbf{Optimise Load:} The integration of a battery energy storage system (BESS) allows diesel engines to always operate at their maximum efficiency point, regardless of speed demand:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Low propulsion demand: Excess energy generated by the engine is used to charge the batteries.
		\item High propulsion demand: Batteries provide additional power (hybrid propulsion), preventing the engine from being overloaded or taken out of its optimal range.
	\end{itemize}
	
\end{itemize}

\section{Classification of Hybrid Propulsion Systems}

The maritime industry has evolved distinct topological approaches to hybridization, each offering specific trade-offs between complexity, cost, redundancy, and efficiency.

\subsection{Series Hybrid Architecture}

In a hybrid series configuration, the mechanical connection between the internal combustion engine and the propeller shaft is completely decoupled, as is shown in \ref{fig:SH}. The vessel is propelled exclusively by electric motors \cite{VolvoPenta}.
The main diesel engines function strictly as generator sets (gensets). They produce electrical energy and fed into a common distribution bus. This bus supplies power to variable frequency drives (VFDs) that control the electric propulsion motors.\\

This system eliminates the inefficiency of diesel engines at low revs, allowing them to always operate at peak performance or delegate the task entirely to the batteries when the load is low \cite{Elica}.

\subsubsection{a) Electric shaft}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \begin{subfigure}{0.5\textwidth}
    	\centering
    	\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{images/chapter03/SeriesHybrid.pdf}
    	\caption{Electric shaft propulsion system.}
    	\label{fig:Series1}
    \end{subfigure}
    \begin{subfigure}{0.5\textwidth}
    	\centering
    	\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{images/chapter03/SeriesHybrid2.jpg}
    	\caption{Electric shaft with electric energy storage system.}
    	\label{fig:Series2}
    \end{subfigure}    
    \caption{Electric Shaft configuration system.}
    \label{fig:Series_ESh}
\end{figure}

The generator transforms the mechanical power of the combustion engine to electrical power. The electric motor on the right transforms the electric power back to mechanical power which is then supplied to the propulsor, as is shown in figure \ref{fig:Series1}. This system is called “electric shaft”, as there is no control input to manipulate the power transmission \cite{Elica}.\\

In electric propulsion systems (both marine and aeronautical)\cite{Elica}, the characteristics of the generator and electric motor define the voltage, current, and frequency. Since the physical design of these machines does not change while they are running, power transmission remains constant, linking the power of the combustion engine to the final rotation of the propeller.\\

Wired connections make it easy to integrate multiple motors and generators, eliminating the rigidity of mechanical shafts; however, the system is vulnerable to overload: if the holding torque of the synchronous machines is exceeded, the system becomes desynchronized, losing power and risking mechanical integrity (cogging torque) \cite{Elica}.\\


The electric shaft system can also be extended with an electric energy storage, to provide additional power during short mission segments, as is shown in figure \ref{fig:Series2}.

The integration of batteries not only improves consumption, but also allows for a physical redesign of the system:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Downsizing:} The combustion engine, electric motors, and wiring can be smaller and lighter, as the storage system compensates for any shortcomings.
	\item \textbf{Electrical Management:} The storage system compensates for reactive power and, through power electronics, eliminates the risk of desynchronization (cogging torque) mentioned above.
\end{itemize}

However, as a vulnerability, the system relies entirely on this compensation to reach its rated power. Therefore, any electrical failure would immediately reduce propulsion capacity \cite{Elica}.

This is the modern standard. Mechanical power is converted to electricity, fed into a DC bus (often 1000V DC), and then inverted back to AC for the propulsion motors, as is shown in figure \ref{fig:Series3}. The insertion of power converters allows the internal combustion engine and the propeller to operate at almost independent rotational speeds. In this way, multiple fixed-pitch rotors can be controlled independently of each other \cite{Elica}.Additionally, the generator set can be resized by adding a BESS connected to the DC link, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:Series4}.\\ 

The series hybrid system is ideal for operations involving frequent stops and low speeds (such as tugboats or ferries), but it loses competitiveness on routes traveled at constant speeds, as the system is between $10\%$ and $15\%$ less efficient than a direct mechanical drive due to multiple energy conversions (from mechanical to electrical and vice versa) \cite{Elica}.

\subsection{Parallel Hybrid Architecture}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/chapter03/ParallelHybrid.jpg}
    \caption{Parallel hybrid propulsion system.}
    \label{fig:Parallel_Hybrid}
\end{figure}

In a parallel hybrid layout, both the electric motor and the combustion engine are mechanically connected to the propeller shaft with a gearbox \cite{Derman}.

In this setup, both the combustion engine and an electric machine (motor/generator) are mechanically connected to the propeller shaft, typically via a reduction gearbox with complex clutching arrangements, as is shown in \ref{fig:PH}. 

In this case, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:Parallel_Hybrid}, the main function of electrical storage is to supplement the power of the main diesel engine, allowing the latter to be sized only for average loads, not for peaks.\\

The following are the operating modes that can be found in the parallel hybrid configuration:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Mechanical Mode (PTO)}: The main diesel engine drives the propeller directly. This is used for heavy towing and high-speed transit. The electric machine may freewheel or act as a generator to charge batteries, mode called Power Take Off (PTO) \cite{Bas}. This is shown in figure \ref{fig:PTO}.
	\begin{figure}[H]
    		\centering
    		\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/chapter03/PTO.pdf}
    		\caption{Energy flow in PTO mode.}
    		\label{fig:PTO}
	\end{figure}
	\item \textbf{Boost Mode (PTI)}: Both the diesel engine and the electric motor provide torque to the shaft simultaneously through the gearbox to the propeller \cite{Bas}. This allows the vessel to achieve a total power output greater than the rating of the main engine alone, providing the "peak" power needed for emergency ship assist maneuvers without requiring a larger main engine. This mode is also called Power Take In (PTI) and is shown in figure \ref{fig:PTI}.
	\begin{figure}[H]
    		\centering
    		\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/chapter03/PTI.pdf}
    		\caption{Energy flow in PTI mode.}
    		\label{fig:PTI}
	\end{figure}
	\item \textbf{Electric Mode (PTH)}: The diesel engine shuts down and the shaft is powered exclusively by the electric motor. This configuration allows for silent, emission-free navigation in ports (0-6 knots), as well as functioning as an emergency system that guarantees the boat's mobility at reduced speed in the event of main engine failure. This mode is also called Power Take Home (PTH) and is shown in figure \ref{fig:PTH}.

\end{itemize}

\section{Grid Distribution}

Typically, electricity has been distributed to other consumers in AC and converted to direct current (DC) for consumers that need it – usually smaller onboard electrical systems, as opposed to the bigger systems such as electric propulsion that require AC \cite{Wartsila2}. Traditional ships use 440V or 690V AC grids. 

But as fully electric and hybrid vessels have become increasingly popular, a new electrical integration concept that uses DC for electricity distributio has become a hot topic. With the integration of batteries (DC sources) and variable speed drives (DC internal), makes AC inefficient.\\

The architectural shift from AC to DC distribution is the technological enabler of modern hybrid vessels.

\subsection{AC Link}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{images/chapter03/AC_Link.pdf}
    \caption{AC Link.}
    \label{fig:AC_Link}
\end{figure}

Historically, AC has been the standard on ships \cite{Nautech}, but it imposes rigid restrictions on the diesel engine:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Mandatory synchronization:} Motors must rotate at a constant speed to maintain stable voltage and frequency in the electrical system.
	\item \textbf{Narrow efficiency range:} Due to this fixed speed, the engine only achieves its optimal fuel consumption (SFOC) in a very limited range, generally around $85\%$ of its rated load.
\end{itemize}

The control strategy for electrical architectures consists of two parts \cite{Geertsma}: 
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{The control of the electrical fixed frequency} network aiming to provide robust power supply to all electrical users.
	\item \textbf{The control of the propulsion} aiming to drive the ship in a certain speed and direction.
\end{itemize}

For onboard power systems, voltages can range from 400 V
in small ships to 11 kV in large vessels with total onboard power
up to 100 MW. Even when docked in a port, a large vessel
may have 10 MW of power requirement \cite{Qazi}.


\subsection{DC Link}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{images/chapter03/DC_Link.pdf}
    \caption{DC Link.}
    \label{fig:DC_Link}
\end{figure}

The Onboard DC Grid concept eliminates the need for bulky propulsion transformers and main AC switchboards. This can reduce the total space occupied by electrical equipment by between 30$\%$ and 75$\%$, freeing up space in a tugboat's engine rooms \cite{ABB}.\\

In an AC grid, generators must run at a fixed speed (e.g., 1800 rpm for 60Hz) to maintain frequency, regardless of load. In a DC grid, the engine speed can be decoupled from the grid frequency. The engine can slow down to idle speeds during low load while still producing the required voltage, with a reduction of the fuel consumption - up to 27$\%$ reduction of spesific fuel oil consumption \cite{ABB}.\\

The industry standard for modern hybrid tugboats has been established around the 1000 V DC grid, considering unipolar and bipolar bus architectures. Generally, low-voltage dc (LVdc) systems are lower than 1000 V DC, with power system capacity in the range of 1–20MW, while medium-voltage dc (MVdc) systems are higher than 1000 V DC, with 20–100 MW \cite{Xu2022}.

\section{Incorporation Grid Distribution}

Electric propulsion and distribution systems can fulfill their role either jointly or separately \cite{Reusser} \cite{SKSul}. 

\subsection{Segregated Power System}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{images/chapter03/Segregated_PS.pdf}
    \caption{Segregated PowerSystem}
    \label{fig:Segregated_PS}
\end{figure}

The segregated power system (SPS) has two separate electrical systems, one dedicated to providing power for ship propulsion and the other reserved for feeding auxiliary loads \cite{Reusser}.\\

In this configuration, energy flows linearly and mechanically:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Direct Drive:} The prime movers (diesel engines) drive the propellers through a physical connection of reduction gears and long shafts.
	\item \textbf{Function of the Gears:} Their role is to transform the high speed of the engine into a speed suitable for the propeller while transferring power, as well as to couple the electric motor with the diesel engine \cite{SKSul}.	
\end{itemize}

In this traditional configuration, propulsion generators and auxiliary generators are separated, which creates three main problems:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Incompatibility:} Operating at different voltages and frequencies, the systems cannot share power with each other.
	\item \textbf{Lack of Flexibility:} Excess power from the main engines cannot be used for ship loads, reducing operational efficiency.
	\item \textbf{Low Performance and Emissions:} Although $90\%$ of installed power is for propulsion \cite{Reusser}, auxiliary generators often operate far from their optimal load due to hotel loads, which unnecessarily increases consumption and pollution.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Integrated Power System}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{images/chapter03/Integrated_PS.pdf}
    \caption{Integrated PowerSystem}
    \label{fig:Integrated_PS}
\end{figure}

In this architecture, the total power required by the propulsion system and service loads is supplied by one or more generators connected to the medium-voltage bus and then distributed to the low-voltage bus via transformers \cite{Reusser}.\\ 

The propulsion motors are fed from power converters, which are connected directly to the main busbar, and the service loads are fed from a low-voltage busbar, using stepdown transformers.\\

The integrated power system (IPS) combines the generation and distribution system into a unified grid, where the generated power is shared between all loads. The greatest advantage of shipboard
IPS comes from power sharing on the common electrical grid, which improves power flexibility and availability \cite{SKSul}. The IPS also offers the ability to manage the generated and delivered power, optimizing the number of running generator sets, which enhances fuel efficiency and reduces emissions.

IPS eliminates the separation between propulsion power and service power, creating a single, shared network \cite{SKSul}:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Unified Network:} All generated energy is centralized, powering both the propulsion engines and the ship's loads.
	\item \textbf{Intelligent Management:} Allows you to optimize how many generators should be turned on based on actual demand, preventing them from operating inefficiently.
\end{itemize}

Thus, the direct benefits come from improved operational flexibility, increased energy availability, optimized fuel use, and reduced pollutant emissions.

This hybrid propulsion system is designed to incorporate electrical and electromechanical elements without removing the original components inside the tugboat, specifically those related to the diesel engines of the conventional propulsion system.\\

Below are some of the features associated with this hybrid tugboat:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Classification of Hybrid Propulsion System:} The propulsion system is a parallel hybrid propulsion system, as it has a gearbox that allows the electric motor and the diesel propulsion engine to be coupled together in order to deliver power to the propeller either mechanically or electrically.
	\item \textbf{Grid Distribution:} The tugboat's internal electrical distribution network is AC, so as not to add an additional stage to the main diesel generator, thus avoiding complexity in control. In addition, the voltage level (400 V) is associated with the levels that can be found in port in order to charge the batteries, taking into account that such (slow charging) can be carried out during the remaining hours outside of service hours.
	\item \textbf{Incorporation Grid Distribution:} The electrical distribution network is considered integrated, given that there is no separation from the propulsion stage. The purpose of this is to enable the main generator to deliver power to the propellers if required.
\end{itemize}

\section{Chapter summary}
This chapter establishes the theoretical and technical foundation for designing an layout for a hybrid propulsion system system. It primarily focuses on how different propulsion setups and electrical grids can improve efficiency and reduce the environmental impact of maritime operations.

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Power Triad}: Hybrid tugboats mostly have three main energy sources: main propulsion engines, auxiliary generators, and the battery energy storage system (BESS).
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{BESS Functions}: Beyond energy storage, the battery bank provides multiple functions such as peak shaving (management of high accelerations), spin reserve (prevention of blackouts), and load optimization (maintaining motors in their efficiency zones).
	\end{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Propulsion Architectures}: Serial architectures (motor and propeller decoupled) and parallel architectures (motor and propeller connected to the shaft) are compared. The parallel configuration is currently the most common in hybrid tugboats, as it allows for variations in operating mode and does not rely on a generator to propel the propellers.
	\item \textbf{Grid Distribution}: A technological shift is taking place from traditional AC links to DC links. DC networks allow motors to operate at variable speeds. However, AC links have the advantage of not requiring a DC charger at the port, making it easier to charge hybrid tugboats.
\end{itemize}

Therefore, the operational advantages of the proposed hybrid system are the use of specific modes, such as towing, free sailing, and standby mode, to optimize efficiency. By using batteries for low-power tasks and the main generator for medium-power tasks, the tugboat can significantly reduce its daily fuel consumption and CO\textsubscript{2} emissions.

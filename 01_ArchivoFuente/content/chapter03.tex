\chapter{Hybrid Tugboats: Requirements and Performance}

This chapter presents the fundamental concepts associated with tugboat hybridization, its most common technological configurations, and the operational benefits associated with the integration of battery banks. The objective is to establish the theoretical and technical basis for the design process carried out in subsequent chapters.

Given the intermittent nature of the load profile and the dependence on diesel propulsion engines, which are oversized for low-load operation, the implementation of the battery energy storage system (BESS) proposed in this thesis is analyzed.

\section{General Power Sources and Energy Storage System}

In general, the hybrid tugboat relies on differents power sources:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Diesel propulsion engines,
	\item Diesel generators (gensets),
	\item Battery Energy Storage System (BESS).
\end{itemize}

The functions of these power sources have changed significantly compared to conventional designs, and the aim is to contribute to both propulsion and hotel loads.

\subsection{Diesel Propulsion Engines}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{images/chapter03/MainPropDieselEngine2.pdf}
    \caption{Diesel propulsion engine used in hybrid tugboats.}
    \label{fig:MainDieselengine2}
\end{figure}
In hybrid tugboats, the diesel propulsion engines are only started when high power is actually needed, as the electric motor handles low-speed maneuvers and idling. This reduces their operating hours, which increases their operational efficiency and reduces their emissions \cite{MAN} \cite{Jayaram}.

As shown in Figure \ref{fig:MainDieselengine2}, the mechanical connection via a reduction gearbox allows an electric motor to be coupled \cite{Bas} \cite{MAN}, which allows the electric motor to transmit its full rated power to the propeller, complementing the work of the diesel propulsion engine \cite{Jayaram}.

\subsection{Diesel Generator(Genset)}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{images/chapter03/AuxGenSet.jpg}
    \caption{Genset inside a hybrid vessel.}
    \label{fig:AuxGenSet}
\end{figure}

In a conventional tugboat, gensets power the ship's service and hotel loads \cite{SKSul}. In a hybrid tugboat, they can play a role in powering the propulsion, services loads and hotel loads. 

They are usually arranged in banks to provide different service powers (for example, four small gensets instead of two large main genset) \cite{Zhang} \cite{Chua}, which makes it possible to use only one genset for low energy demand and the rest for higher demands.

As shown in Figure \ref{fig:AuxGenSet}, a genset can be used to \cite{Hofman}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Deliver power to the propellers via a series connection, when less power than the rated power of the diesel propulsion engines is required.
	\item Without connecting to the propellers, it allows the batteries to be charged when there is no possibility of charging them in port. 
	\item Power supply to the tugboat services and hotel loads.
\end{itemize}
 
\subsection{Battery Bank}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.3\linewidth]{images/chapter03/BattBank.jpg}
    \caption{Battery bank made with multiple cells.}
    \label{fig:BattBank_scheme}
\end{figure}

The function of the BESS shown in the Figure \ref{fig:BattBank_scheme} it is not just about storing energy for the propulsion system; it also complements the operation of the diesel propulsion engines and gensets in a hybrid configuration. As mentioned in section \ref{sec:Roles}, these are some of the complementary functions that the BESS can provide in a hybrid tugboat.

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Peak Shaving:} The use of a BESS solves the inefficiencies of diesel propulsion engines when faced with sudden changes in power. Instead of forcing the propulsion engines to accelerate rapidly—which causes thermal stress and polluting emissions (black smoke)—the battery supplies energy instantly. 
	\item \textbf{Spinning Reverse:} Traditionally, a genset is kept running and synchronized with the ship’s electrical system, operating at low load. Its purpose is to be ready to take over the load instantly if another generator fails, thereby preventing a blackout.
In the case of a battery energy storage system (BESS), the battery can take on this role instantly: unlike an internal combustion engine, which needs time to accelerate and take on the load, the power electronics of a BESS can deliver its rated power within milliseconds. In addition, the BESS allows for the shutdown of those gensets that previously had to be kept running for safety reasons. This translates into significant fuel savings and a reduction in maintenance hours.
	\item \textbf{Optimise Load:} The integration of a BESS could allows diesel propulsion engines to operate at their maximum efficiency point, regardless of speed demand:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Low propulsion demand: Excess energy generated by the diesel propulsion engine is used to charge the batteries.
		\item High propulsion demand: Batteries provide additional power (hybrid propulsion), preventing the propulsion engine from being overloaded or taken out of its optimal range.
	\end{itemize}
	
\end{itemize}

\section{Classification of Hybrid Propulsion Systems}

The maritime industry has developed different topological approaches to hybridization, each offering specific trade-offs between complexity, cost, redundancy, and efficiency.

\subsection{Series Hybrid Architecture}

In a hybrid series configuration, the mechanical connection between the internal combustion engine and the propeller shaft is mechanically decoupled, as is shown in \ref{fig:SH}. The vessel is propelled exclusively by electric motors \cite{VolvoPenta}.
Gensets produce electrical energy and fed into a common distribution bus. This bus supplies power to variable frequency drives (VFDs) that control the electric propulsion motors.\\


\subsubsection{a) Electric shaft}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \begin{subfigure}{0.5\textwidth}
    	\centering
    	\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{images/chapter03/SeriesHybrid.pdf}
    	\caption{Electric shaft propulsion system.}
    	\label{fig:Series1}
    \end{subfigure}
    \begin{subfigure}{0.5\textwidth}
    	\centering
    	\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{images/chapter03/SeriesHybrid2.jpg}
    	\caption{Electric shaft with electric energy storage system.}
    	\label{fig:Series2}
    \end{subfigure}    
    \caption{Electric Shaft configuration system \cite{Elica}.}
    \label{fig:Series_ESh}
\end{figure}

The genset transforms the mechanical power of the diesel engine to electrical power. The electric motor transforms the electric power back to mechanical power, which is then supplied to the propeller, as is shown in figure \ref{fig:Series1}. This system is called “electric shaft” \cite{Elica}.\\

The electric propulsion system uses the fixed properties of the genset and motor to establish constant power transmission between the diesel engine and the propeller, based on defined voltage, current, and frequency parameters \cite{Elica}.\\

Wired connections make it easy to integrate multiple motors and generators; however, the system is vulnerable to overload: if the holding torque of the synchronous machines is exceeded, the system becomes desynchronized, losing power and risking mechanical integrity \cite{Elica}.\\


The electric shaft system can also be extended with an electric energy storage, to provide additional power during short services, as is shown in figure \ref{fig:Series2} (a BESS in this case).

The integration of batteries not only improves consumption, but also allows for a physical redesign of the system:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Downsizing:} Gensets, electric motors and wiring can be smaller and lighter, as the energy storage system compensates for any shortcomings.
	\item \textbf{Electrical Management:} The energy storage system compensates for reactive power and, through power electronics, eliminates the risk of desynchronization.
\end{itemize}

However, as a vulnerability, the system relies entirely on this compensation to reach its rated power. Therefore, any electrical failure would immediately reduce propulsion capacity \cite{Elica}.

Mechanical power is converted to electricity, fed into a DC bus (often 1000V DC), and then inverted back to AC for the electric motors, as is shown in figure \ref{fig:Series3}.  Additionally, the genset can be resized by adding a BESS connected to the DC link, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:Series4}. The idea behind this is to ensure that the genset operates at its optimal efficiency and the BESS operates at lower power levels or helps supply the additional power needed by the genset, if required by the propeller.\\ 

The series hybrid system loses competitiveness on routes traveled at constant speeds, as the system is between $10\%$ and $15\%$ less efficient than a direct mechanical drive due to multiple energy conversions (from mechanical to electrical and vice versa) \cite{Elica}.

\subsection{Parallel Hybrid Architecture}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/chapter03/ParallelHybrid.jpg}
    \caption{Scheme of a parallel hybrid propulsion system.}
    \label{fig:Parallel_Hybrid}
\end{figure}

In a parallel hybrid layout, both the electric motor and the diesel engine are mechanically connected to the propeller through a gearbox, as is shown in \ref{fig:PH}. The main idea is to perform various functions related to the power flow to the propellers like PTI and PTO, as mentioned in section \ref{sec:Diesel_Hyb_Sys}   \cite{Derman}.

In this case, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:Parallel_Hybrid}, the main function of energy storage system is to supplement the power of the diesel propulsion engine, allowing the latter to be sized only for average loads, not for peaks.\\

The following is a list of the operating modes found in the parallel hybrid configuration. Although these were mentioned in Section \ref{sec:Diesel_Hyb_Sys}, further details will be provided here:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Mechanical Mode (PTO)}: The diesel propulsion engine drives the propeller directly. This is used for heavy towing and high-speed transit. The electric motor can operate in freewheel mode or act as an electric generator to charge the batteries in the event that the diesel propulsion engine produces excess power; this mode is called power take-off (PTO) \cite{Bas}. This is shown in figure \ref{fig:PTO}.
	\begin{figure}[H]
    		\centering
    		\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/chapter03/PTO.pdf}
    		\caption{Energy flow in PTO mode.}
    		\label{fig:PTO}
	\end{figure}
	\item \textbf{Boost Mode (PTI)}: Both the diesel propulsion engine and the electric motor provide torque to the propeller simultaneously through the gearbox \cite{Bas}. This allows the vessel to achieve a total power output greater than the rating of the diesel propulsion engine alone, providing the "peak" power needed for emergency ship assist maneuvers without requiring a larger diesel engine. This mode is also called Power Take In (PTI) and is shown in figure \ref{fig:PTI}.
	\begin{figure}[H]
    		\centering
    		\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/chapter03/PTI.pdf}

    		\label{fig:PTI}
	\end{figure}
	
	\item \textbf{Electric Mode (PTH)}: The diesel propulsion engine shuts down and the propeller is powered exclusively by the electric motor. This configuration allows for silent, emission-free navigation in ports (0-6 knots), as well as functioning as an emergency system that guarantees the mobility of the tugboat at reduced speed in the event of diesel engine failure. This mode is also called Power Take Home (PTH) and is shown in figure \ref{fig:PTH}.

\section{Grid Distribution}

Typically, electricity has been distributed to other consumers in AC and converted to direct current (DC) for consumers that need it – usually smaller onboard electrical systems, as opposed to the bigger systems such as electric propulsion that require AC \cite{Wartsila2}. Traditional ships use 400V or 690V AC grids. 

But as fully electric and hybrid vessels have become increasingly popular, a new electrical integration concept that uses DC for electricity distribution has become a hot topic. With the integration of batteries (DC sources) and variable speed drives (DC internal), makes AC inefficient.\\

The architectural shift from AC to DC distribution is the technological enabler of modern hybrid vessels.

\subsection{AC Link}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{images/chapter03/AC_Link.jpg}
    \caption{Scheme of AC Link into the tugboat.}
    \label{fig:AC_Link}
\end{figure}

Historically, AC has been the standard on ships \cite{Nautech}, but it imposes rigid restrictions on the diesel engine:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Mandatory synchronization:} Gensets must rotate at a constant speed to maintain stable voltage and frequency in the electrical system.
	\item \textbf{Narrow efficiency range:} Due to this fixed speed, the engine only achieves its optimal fuel consumption (BSFC) in a very limited range, generally around $85\%$ of its rated load.
\end{itemize}

The control strategy for electrical architectures consists of two parts \cite{Geertsma}: 
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{The control of the electrical fixed frequency} network aiming to provide robust power supply to all electrical users.
	\item \textbf{The control of the propulsion} aiming to drive the ship in a certain speed and direction.
\end{itemize}

For onboard power systems, voltages can range from 400 V
in small ships to 11 kV in large vessels with total onboard power
up to 100 MW. Even when docked in a port, a large vessel
may have 10 MW of power requirement for hotel and services loads \cite{Qazi}.


\subsection{DC Link}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{images/chapter03/DC_Link.pdf}
    \caption{Scheme of DC Link into the tugboat.}
    \label{fig:DC_Link}
\end{figure}

The onboard DC Grid concept eliminates the need for bulky propulsion transformers and a main AC switchboards. This can reduce the total space occupied by electrical equipment by between 30$\%$ and 75$\%$, freeing up space in a tugboat's engine rooms \cite{ABB}.\\

DC networks allow the genset speed to vary according to the load, unlike the constant speed required in AC. This decoupling optimizes consumption at low demand, achieving fuel savings of up to 27$\%$ \cite{ABB}.\\

Hybrid architectures typically use 1000 V DC networks. These are categorized as LVdc (up to 1000 V and 20 MW) or MVdc (above 1000 V and 20 MW) systems, using unipolar or bipolar bus architectures \cite{Xu2022}.

\section{Incorporation Grid Distribution}

Electric propulsion and distribution systems can fulfill their role either jointly or separately \cite{Reusser} \cite{SKSul}. 

\subsection{Segregated Power System}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{images/chapter03/Segregated_PS.pdf}
    \caption{Segregated PowerSystem}
    \label{fig:Segregated_PS}
\end{figure}

The segregated power system (SPS) has two separate electrical systems, one dedicated to providing power for ship propulsion and the other reserved for feeding service and hotel loads \cite{Reusser}.\\

In this configuration, energy flows linearly and mechanically:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Direct Drive:} Diesel propulsion engines drive the propellers through a physical connection of reduction gears and long shafts.
	\item \textbf{Function of gearboxes:} Their role is to transform the high speed of the engine into a speed suitable for the propeller while transferring power, as well as to couple the electric motor with the diesel propulsion engine \cite{SKSul}.	
\end{itemize}

In this traditional configuration, gensets and diesel propulsion engines are separated, which creates some problems:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Lack of Flexibility:} Excess power from the diesel propulsion engines cannot be used for ship loads, reducing operational efficiency.
	\item \textbf{Low Performance and Emissions:} Although $90\%$ of installed power is for propulsion \cite{Reusser}, gensets often operate far from their optimal load due to service and hotel loads, which unnecessarily increases consumption and pollution.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Integrated Power System}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{images/chapter03/Integrated_PS.pdf}
    \caption{Integrated PowerSystem}
    \label{fig:Integrated_PS}
\end{figure}

In this architecture, the total power required by the propulsion system and service and hotel loads is supplied by one or more gensets connected to the main voltage bus and then distributed to the via transformers \cite{Reusser}.\\ 

The propulsion electric motors are fed from power drives, which are connected directly to the main busbar, and the service and hotel loads are fed using stepdown transformers.\\

The integrated power system (IPS) system integrates generation and distribution into a common electrical network. This energy exchange dramatically improves operational flexibility and system availability compared to traditional configurations \cite{SKSul}. By actively managing the power delivered, IPS minimizes the use of generators, optimizing consumption and environmental impact.

Therefore, IPS eliminates the separation between propulsion power and service and hotel power, creating a single, shared network \cite{SKSul}:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Unified Network:} All generated energy is centralized, powering both the propulsion engines and the ship's loads.
	\item \textbf{Energy Management System (EMS):} Allows you to optimize how gensets should be turned on based on actual demand, preventing them from operating inefficiently.
\end{itemize}

Thus, the direct benefits come from improved operational flexibility, increased energy availability, optimized fuel use, and reduced pollutant emissions.

A hybridization of the tugboat propulsion system seen in Section \ref{sec:Tugboat} is proposed. The general layout of the hybrid propulsion system is presented in Figure \ref{fig:Esquema_General}.

    \label{fig:Esquema_General}

This hybrid propulsion system is designed to incorporate electrical and electromechanical elements without removing the original components inside the tugboat, specifically those related to the diesel engines of the conventional propulsion system.\\

Below are some of the features associated with this hybrid tugboat:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Classification of Hybrid Propulsion System:} The propulsion system is primarily a parallel hybrid system, with a secondary operating mode that allows for a series hybrid configuration. This is because it features a gearbox that allows the electric motor and the diesel propulsion engine to be coupled to transmit power to the propeller, either mechanically or electrically, and, independently, can drive the generator set to supply power to the propulsion system.
	\item \textbf{Grid Distribution:} The tugboat's internal electrical distribution network is AC, so as not to add an additional stage to the genset and avoiding the installation of an AC/DC charger on board, thus avoiding complexity in control. In addition, the voltage level (400 V) is associated with the levels that can be found in port in order to charge the batteries, taking into account that such (slow charging) can be carried out during the remaining hours outside of service hours.
	\item \textbf{Integration Grid Distribution:} The electrical distribution network is considered integrated, given that there is no separation from the propulsion stage. The purpose of this is to enable the genset to deliver power to the propellers if required.
\end{itemize}

\section{Chapter summary}
This chapter establishes the theoretical and technical foundation for designing a layout for a hybrid propulsion system. It primarily focuses on how different propulsion setups and electrical grids can improve efficiency and reduce the environmental impact of maritime operations.

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Different power sources}: Hybrid tugboats mostly have three main energy sources: diesel propulsion engines, gensets and BESS.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{BESS Functions}: Beyond energy storage, the battery bank provides multiple functions such as peak shaving (management of high accelerations), spin reserve (prevention of blackouts), and load optimization (maintaining motors in their efficiency zones).
	\end{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Propulsion Architectures}: Series architectures (where the engine and propeller are mechanically decoupled) and parallel architectures (where the engine and propeller are connected to the same shaft). Although the first configuration is generally less complex than the second one, each has its advantages in hybrid propulsion systems, allowing for the best of both options to be leveraged when considering a series/parallel configuration.
	\item \textbf{Grid Distribution}: DC internal grids allow motors to operate at variable speeds and are an option that improves the efficiency of the electrical system. However, internal AC grid have the advantage of directly integrating the gensets located inside the tugboat, without the need for an onboard charger (AC/DC) on the tugboat or a onshore charger (DC/DC), which could increase the acquisition cost of the hybrid system.
\end{itemize}

Therefore, the operational advantages of the proposed hybrid system are the use of specific modes, such as \textit{Towing Mode}, \textit{Free Sailing Mode} and \textit{StandBy Mode}, to optimize efficiency. By using batteries for low-power tasks and the genset for medium-power tasks, the tugboat can significantly reduce its daily fuel consumption and CO\textsubscript{2} emissions. Thus, the only decision left to make is whether to cover the low-power range fully or partially with the power delivered by the battery bank.

\chapter{Tugboats: Description and Operation}

Tugboats are small but powerful vessels designed to assist in docking ships or moving structures without their own propulsion. Due to their robust engineering and high power, this chapter analyzes their technical and operational characteristics based on a real service profile in Chile, in order to identify the key aspects necessary for their hybridization.

A tugboat is technically defined as a type of vessel specifically designed to assist other ships in moving by providing a controlled amount of force. They are essential vessels in ports, as is shown in fig. \ref{fig:Tug1} and \ref{fig:Tug2}, where they assist in the movement of floating objects and in emergency tasks \cite{INEAL}.

Maritime towing is legally defined as the action of pulling a vessel or floating device that lacks its own propulsion or navigational autonomy. This activity has a dual function: 
\begin{itemize}
\item Precision assistance in port maneuvers,
\item deep-sea towing or salvage.
\end{itemize}
Both functions require specific design engineering and system redundancy to manage the different operational risks \cite{Bruce}.

The importance of tugboats has grown exponentially with the phenomenon of naval gigantism \cite{Murimar}. Large modern ships, such as cruise ships and oil tankers, have tremendous momentum (inertia) that prevents them from stopping quickly or maneuvering effectively on their own in narrow channels or docks \cite{ESC_Group}.   

The tugboat is essential for managing the ship's inertia and ensuring operational safety during maneuvers and contingencies \cite{Bruce}. Operational efficiency depends on fuel consumption relative to load, factors that are conditioned by the configuration of the propulsion and energy system. The efficiency of conventional propulsion systems varies between 38$\%$ and 41$\%$ for a power range between 100 kW and 2,500 kW. For vessels with higher power ratings, efficiency increases to 48$\%$ \cite{Griffiths} \cite{Wik}.

Gross Tonnage (GT) measures the “total inside space” of the ship, including all areas like cargo holds, engine rooms, and crew spaces. It’s about “volume”, not weight \cite{Wartsila} \cite{chinarubberfender}.

DWT refers to weight and indicates how much the ship can safely carry. It is expressed in long tons of 2,240 pounds (1,016.0469088 kilograms) \cite{Britannica}.

\subsubsection{c) Displacement Tonnage}
Displacement tonnage is used to define the size of naval ships. It refers to the weight of the volume of water displaced by a vessel in normal seagoing condition \cite{Britannica}.

\section{Technical Characteristics of the Propulsion System}

% Explicar las características mecánicas y eléctricas que determinan cómo debe diseñarse el sistema híbrido.

To understand the work of a tugboat as a maritime unit, it is essential to analyze both its essential operational characteristics and the elements of its propulsion system that enable it to operate.

\subsection{Bollard Pull - BP}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[scale=1]{images/chapter02/Bollard_pull.png}
    \caption{The bollard pull test to measure a vessel's maximum towing capacity \cite{ESC_Group}.}
    \label{fig:BollardPullTug}
\end{figure}

Bollard pull (BP) is the fundamental metric for quantifying a tugboat's pulling power.

Equivalent to automotive torque, BP measures the static traction of the vessel. The process involves securing the vessel to a fixed point and recording the tension of the line using load cells at full thrust, as is shown in the figure \ref{fig:BollardPullTug}.   

The BP measures load capacity in units such as kN or tf. All tugboats require a fixed point certification issued by classification societies (such as ABS or BV) to validate their operability \cite{Murimar}.  

The tugboat's design seeks to maximize its pulling power in relation to its dimensions. A common method of achieving this is to install a nozzle around the propeller, thereby optimizing propulsion efficiency. \cite{Murimar}. 

\subsection{Propulsion System for Maneuvering}
The maneuverability of a tugboat, which directly influences its operational efficiency and safety, is intrinsically linked to the design of its propulsion system.

\subsubsection{a) Conventional Propulsion} 

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{images/chapter02/ConventionalTug.pdf}
    \caption{Conventional propulsion: A tug fitted with fixed propellers, single or twin screw (left or right handed) and single rudders with fixed nozzles \cite{Gokay}.}
    \label{fig:ConventionalProp}
\end{figure}

It uses a standard diesel engine connected to a propeller, as is shown in the figure \ref{fig:ConventionalProp}. It is a proven and simple system, with good efficiency in linear towing tasks within ports and coastal areas. However, it offers limited lateral maneuverability compared to more advanced systems \cite{MJimenez}.

\subsubsection{b) Azimuthal Stern Drive (ASD)} 


\begin{figure}[H]
    \begin{subfigure}{0.5\textwidth}
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{images/chapter02/ASD1Tug.pdf}
    \caption{Azimuth Stern Drive (ASD): A tug fitted with \\two azimuth thrusters in nozzles at the stern, \\capable to direct all its thrust in any direction \cite{Gokay}.}
    \label{fig:ASD1_Tug}
    \end{subfigure}
    \begin{subfigure}{0.5\textwidth}
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{images/chapter02/ASD2Tug.pdf}
    \caption{ASD Tractor: A tug with azimuth thrusters \\located generally forward off the midship and a rudder-shaped fin at aft side. \cite{Gokay}.}
    \label{fig:ASD2_Tug}
    \end{subfigure}    
    \caption{ASD Tugs representations.}
    \label{fig:ASD_Tugs}
\end{figure}

These are rotary thrusters, where a motor is capable of directing all its thrust in any direction \cite{MJimenez}. Its two independent propellers maximize maneuverability, allowing the ship to turn 360° on its own length \cite{JFarina}, as is shown in the figure \ref{fig:ASD1_Tug}. The only difference with conventional tugboats are the modifications to the stern and structure to house the propellers and withstand the forces exerted by the nozzle rudder, which are different from those exerted by a conventional rudder and propeller \cite{JFarina}. 

%Z-Drive propulsion systems optimize power transmission and provide superior maneuverability when towing thanks to their efficient mechanical configuration \cite{MJimenez}.\\ 

An ASD Tractor Tug generally has two fixed pitch azimuth propellers in the center of the tugboat, as is shown in the figure \ref{fig:ASD2_Tug}.

To compensate for the loss of directional control caused by very forward-mounted thrusters, a stern fin is installed to stabilize the trajectory \cite{Shiphandling}.

\subsubsection{c) Voith-Schneider Propulsion (VSP)} 

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{images/chapter02/VSPTug.pdf}
    \caption{Voith-Schneider Propeller (VSP) propulsion: A tug with VSP generally located forward off the midship and a rudder-shaped fin at aft side \cite{Gokay}.}
    \label{fig:VSP_Prop}
\end{figure}

These harbor tugs have been equipped with \textit{VoithSchneider} propellers, a unique type of propulsion system consisting of oscillating blades that allow them to have total, targeted control over the vessel without having to adjust the orientation of the main engine or propellers \cite{MJimenez}. This system, as is shown in the figure \ref{fig:VSP_Prop}, offers omnidirectional thrust and total steering control, resulting positioning accuracy.

This propulsion system allows for highly precise operation in any direction due to its agility, making it ideal for rescue operations as well as in ports crowded with high-density maritime traffic \cite{MJimenez}.

\subsubsection{d) Propulsion Comparison }

The transition from a conventional system to an azimuth system, and even more so to a Voith-Schneider system, involves an increase in engineering complexity and acquisition cost, but this is fully justified by the need to operate with high precision and safety in confined spaces or under adverse weather conditions. The design of these advanced systems is crucial for safety, as they allow the tugboat to apply the required force (BP) without compromising lateral stability and minimizing the risk of capsizing.

\subsubsection{a) Schottel system}


A Schottel system is a type of azimuth thruster that combines propulsion and steering functions into a single unit.
Unlike a conventional system (where you have a fixed propeller shaft and a rudder behind it), the Schottel system allows the entire propeller unit to rotate 360 degrees around its vertical axis, as is shown in the figure \ref{fig:schottel}.
Because it can direct the water flow in any horizontal direction, the tugboat does not need a rudder to turn; it simply directs the thrust toward where it needs to go \cite{inter}.


\subsubsection{b) Voith-Schneider System (Cycloidal Propeller)}

This propeller system consists of a rotor that rotates on a vertical shaft fixed to the hull approximately at its pivot point, as is shown in the figure \ref{fig:VSP_propeller}. It is equipped with several blades that pivot on vertical axes controlled from the bridge by the steering control. This device is responsible for directing the thrust in the opposite direction to the desired thrust. The mechanism is synchronized to center the thrust at a single point.



\section{Naval Maneuvers and Assistance Techniques}

Towing operations are governed by strict procedures that seek to maximize safety in dynamic and often restricted maritime and port areas.	

\subsection{Docking and Undocking Procedures}

The assistance of tugboats is crucial during docking and undocking maneuvers. The undocking maneuver can be as laborious as the docking maneuver and must be planned in advance. The objective is to leave the assisted vessel clear of the dock, in a safe position that allows the rudder and propeller to be effective so that the vessel can gain control and momentum.

It is essential to consider the effects of winds and currents. Strong winds from outside can add to the effects of the engine in reverse during undocking, creating a dangerous situation that can violently push the stern or bow toward the dock. The ability of the highly maneuverable tugboat to apply instantaneous lateral force reduces the time the assisted vessel is exposed to the combined and unfavorable action of wind and propulsion, mitigating the risk of collision. If the wind action is severe, tugboat assistance is mandatory, especially if the actions of the assisted vessel's capstan are insufficient.

\subsection{Tugboat Performance}

There are different ship-handling operations that tugboats perform to help large ships enter or leave different ports, such as those listed below.

\subsubsection{a) Tugboat Working on a Beam or Over a Cable}

Line towing allows the tugboat to assist the ship from a distance, adjusting its function (pull or hold) according to the connection point of the rope, as is shwon in the figure \ref{fig:OverCable}. Trailing-line towing optimizes power transfer and prevents collisions, but its large space requirement prevents use in restricted areas \cite{IngeMarino}.

In this procedure, the tugboat rests its bow on the side of the vessel it is assisting and pushes it in a direction that is essentially perpendicular to the centerline (the straight line running from stern to bow), as is shwon in the figure \ref{fig:Bow}. It is common in this procedure for the tugboat to be secured to the ship with 1, 2, and 3 mooring lines, which prevents relative slippage between the two vessels during the maneuver \cite{IngeMarino}.

\begin{figure}[H]

In this procedure, the tugboat is positioned alongside the ship and roughly parallel to it, moored to the ship by several ropes that ensure the transmission of force, as is shown in the figura \ref{fig:alongside}. This procedure is generally used to maneuver ships that do not have sufficient propulsion, in confined spaces and in very calm waters \cite{IngeMarino}.

\subsection{Brake-Specific Fuel Consumption (BSFC)}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{images/chapter02/BSFC.pdf}
    \caption{Brake-specific fuel consumption of a diesel engine model Cat 3516 B.}
    \label{fig:BSFC}
\end{figure}

To analyze the operating profile, it is necessary to introduce the concept of \textbf{brake-specific fuel consumption (BSFC)}. BSFC is a measure of an engine's fuel efficiency, calculated as the amount of fuel consumed per unit of power produced over a specific time \cite{Britannica}. 

The BSFC as a function of output power is illustrated in fig. \ref{fig:BSFC} for a typical engine used in tugboats. The engine achieves its maximum efficiency at power levels below the rated power, where the specific fuel consumption (BSFC) is lowest ($75–80\%$ of the rated power). Thus, generally it can be seen that operation at low power and at rated power increases fuel consumption per unit of energy, which reduces efficiency, with maximum efficiency occurring between $75\%$ and $80\%$ (the operating range with minimal emissions) \cite{Reusser2}.

The relative efficiency evaluates how the conventional engine is working taking as a reference the optimal efficiency at the nominal operating point as shown in eq. \eqref{eq:rel_eff} \cite{LSZ}. At this operating point the relative efficiency is 100$\%$ reducing its value if the engine works at lower levels of power.

\begin{align}\label{eq:rel_eff}
    \eta_\text{rel} &= \frac{\text{BSFC}_\text{nom}}{\text{BSFC}}
\end{align}

With the information provided by the engine in Figure \ref{fig:BSFC}, it is then possible to analyze how relative efficiency varies based on the load profile. This is shown in Figures \ref{fig:1M_releff} and \ref{fig:2M_releff}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/chapter02/1Maneuver_RelEff.pdf}
    \caption{Relative efficiency of a convencitonal tugboat: one maneuver.}
    \label{fig:1M_releff}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]

\end{figure}

It can be observed in figures \ref{fig:1M_releff} y \ref{fig:2M_releff} that as the tugboat operates close to the nominal power point of its diesel engines, its relative efficiency improves. This indicates optimal fuel consumption during those periods. Conversely, at lower operating points, the engine consumes more fuel relative to the energy produced.

\subsection{Generated Emissions and Fuel Consumption}

To calculate emissions, the following formula relates brake power (${P}_{b}$), BSFC, usage time (${t}_{u}$), and a carbon factor (CF)(whose value is equal to 3.206 [g- CO\textsubscript{2}/g-FO])\cite{MEPC}:

\begin{align}\label{eq:emiss}
    \text{Emissions} &= \frac{\text{BSFC} \cdot {P}_{b} \cdot {t}_{u} \cdot \text{CF}}{10^{-6}} \quad \text{[t CO\textsubscript{2}]}
\end{align}

With equation \eqref{eq:emiss}, you can find out the amount of emissions produced by the tugboat for each load profile. This can be seen in Figures \ref{fig:1M_emiss} and \ref{fig:2M_emiss}, where fuel consumption per hour of tugboat service can also be represented, considering the density $d$ of diesel, which in this case corresponds to $832.5$ [g/L] \cite{lgc} and fuel consumption is related to emissions as follows:

\begin{align}\label{eq:fuel}
\text{BSFC} &=\frac{d\cdot \text{Fuel}}{{P}_{b}} \to \text{Emissions} = \frac{\text{Fuel} \cdot d \cdot {t}_{u}\cdot \text{CF}}{10^{-6}} \quad \text{[t CO\textsubscript{2}]} 
\end{align}

\section{Chapter Summary}


This chapter established the fundamental technical and operational framework required to understand the role of tugboats in the maritime industry, serving as the baseline for the proposed hybrid propulsion design.


The tugboat is defined as an essential assistance vessel designed to aid the mooring, berthing, and maneuvering of larger ships. The chapter classified these vessels into three primary categories based on their operational domain:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Harbour Tugs}: Designed for agility and restricted drafts within ports.
	\item \textbf{Offshore/SAR Tugs}: Built for open ocean robustness and long-range autonomy.
	\item \textbf{Escort Tugs}: High-power vessels specialized in safety for hazardous cargo in high-risk zones.
\end{itemize}


A critical analysis of propulsion engineering was presented, highlighting Bollard Pull (BP) as the primary metric for quantifying a tug's static pulling force. The comparison of propulsion systems revealed distinct trade-offs:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Conventional Propulsion}: Offers linear efficiency but limited lateral maneuverability.
	\item \textbf{Azimuth Stern Drive (ASD)}: Utilizes rotatable thrusters (Z-Drive) for 360-degree maneuverability, a standard for modern port operations.
	\item \textbf{Voith-Schneider (VSP)}: Provides omnidirectional thrust and extreme precision for congested environments.  
\end{itemize}


The chapter concluded with a case study of a tugboat operating in Puerto Montt, Chile. By analyzing the Brake-Specific Fuel Consumption (BSFC), the study identified a significant inefficiency in traditional propulsion system:

\begin{itemize}
	\item Diesel engines achieve optimal efficiency (low BSFC) within a range close to their rated power.
	\item The operational profile of a tugboat is highly variable; during low-load periods (e.g., transit or standby), the engines operate at low relative efficiency, leading to higher fuel consumption per kWh produced.
	\item Data indicated that in low power ranges (180kW – 1270kW), the relative efficiency drops significantly, yet this range can account for up to $38\%$ of daily fuel consumption in single-maneuver scenarios
\end{itemize}

This analysis demonstrates that the intermittency of the load profile and the reliance on oversized diesel engines for low-load tasks result in excessive GHG emissions, justifying the need for the hybrid energy storage system proposed in this thesis.

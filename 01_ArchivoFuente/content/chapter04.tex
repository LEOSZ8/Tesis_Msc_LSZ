\chapter{Design of the battery bank for a hybrid propulsion system}

This chapter presents the preliminary design process for the battery bank required for the tugboat's hybrid propulsion system. The energy capacity, power requirements, and operating constraints are determined, and then different storage technologies are evaluated from a technical and economic perspective.


\section{Energy Capacity (kWh)}
To calculate the net capacity required for the battery bank design, various factors that provide context must be considered \cite{IEEE1184} \cite{ieng}. 

The fundamental energy requirement, denoted as $E_{\text{load}}$ is calculated by integrating the power demand over the duration of the design mission.

$$E_{\text{load}} = \int_{0}^{t} (P_{\text{propulsion}}(t) + P_{\text{hotel}}(t)) \,dt$$

For discrete phases, this summation becomes:

$$E_{\text{load}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P_i \times t_i$$

Where:

\begin{itemize}
	\item $P_i$ is the total electrical load at the DC bus (propulsion + hotel) in kilowatts (kW) for phase $i$.
	\item $t_i$ is the duration of phase $i$ in hours.
\end{itemize}

This value, $E_{\text{load}}$, represents the energy that must be delivered to the propellers and the hotel loads. However, calculating the installed battery capacity ($E_{\text{installed}}$) requires expanding this value to account for the physical and chemical realities of the energy storage system.

This transformation is achieved through the application of specific sizing factors.

\subsubsection{a) System Efficiency Factor ($\eta_{sys}$)}

Energy stored in the electrochemical cells must traverse a electrical path to reach the propulsion motor. Each component in this chain extracts an efficiency penalty (heat loss).

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Coulombic/Voltaic Efficiency} ($\eta_{bat}$): Li-ion batteries are highly efficient, but internal resistance ($R_{int}$) causes $I^2R$ losses during discharge. Typical round-trip efficiency is $92-96\%$; discharge efficiency is generally taken as $0.96$ to $0.98$ \cite{BattUniversity}.
	\item \textbf{DC/DC Converters} $\eta_{conv}$:  Many hybrid architectures utilize DC/DC converters to decouple the battery voltage from the main DC bus voltage. These typically operate at $0.97$ to $0.98$ efficiency.
	\item \textbf{Variable Frequency Drives (VFD)} $\eta_{vfd}$: This inverters for electric motors  converting DC bus power to AC for the motors operate at $0.96$ to $0.98$ efficiency.
	\item \textbf{Electric Motor} $\eta_{motor}$: Permanent Magnet (PM) machines are preferred for hybrid tugs due to their high efficiency at partial loads compared to induction motors. PM motor efficiency is typically $0.95$ to $0.97$.
\end{enumerate}

To size the battery based on shaft power demand, the cumulative efficiency is:

\begin{equation}
\eta_{sys} = \eta_{bat} \times \eta_{conv} \times \eta_{vfd} \times \eta_{motor}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{b) Maximum Depth of Discharge ($DOD_{max}$) and Reserve Margins}

The Depth of Discharge (DOD) is the percentage of the battery's capacity that has been discharged relative to its total capacity. While Li-ion chemistries can be discharged to $100\%$ ($0\%$ SOC), doing so drastically reduces cycle life and increases the risk of cell degradation.

\begin{itemize}
	\item  \textbf{Cycle Life Correlation}: The relationship between DOD and cycle life is non-linear. For example, an NMC cell might offer 2,000 cycles at $100\%$ DOD but 5,000 cycles at $80\%$ DOD.
	\item \textbf{Operational Reserve}: Marine safety standards often dictate a "reserve energy" capability. For a tugboat, this might mean retaining enough energy after a standard operation to perform emergency maneuvering or firefighting for a specified duration.
	\item \textbf{Voltage Sag}: At very low SOC ($<10\%$), the cell voltage drops rapidly. To maintain the required bus voltage and power delivery, the current must increase, leading to higher thermal stress.
\end{itemize}

Standard marine practice utilizes a Maximum DOD of $80\%$. This implies the battery operates between $10\%$ and $90\%$ SOC.

\begin{equation}
\text{Factor}_{\text{DOD}} = \frac{1}{DOD_{max}} 
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{c) Battery Aging Factor ($k_{aging}$)}

Batteries degrade over time due to two primary mechanisms:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Calendar Aging:} Loss of capacity due to chemical decomposition of the electrolyte and SEI (Solid Electrolyte Interphase) layer growth, occurring even when the battery is idle. This is temperature and SOC dependent.
	\item \textbf{Cyclic Aging:} Mechanical stress on the electrode materials during ion insertion/extraction, leading to cracking and active material loss. This is driven by throughput (Ah) and C-rates.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{End of Life (EOL) Definition}: In the maritime industry, a battery system is considered to have reached its End of Life (EOL) when its capacity fades to $80\%$ of its original nominal capacity (Beginning of Life, BOL). The vessel must be capable of performing its design mission on the last day of the battery's life (e.g., Year 10). Therefore, the battery must be oversized at installation so that the faded capacity still meets the requirement.



\begin{equation}
k_{aging} = \frac{1}{SOH_{EOL}}
\end{equation}


\subsubsection{d) Temperature Correction Factor ($k_{temp}$)}

Battery performance is inextricably linked to temperature. The standard rating is typically given at 25°C.

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Low Temperature Effects}: At temperatures below 15°C, the viscosity of the electrolyte increases, and ion mobility decreases. This manifests as increased internal resistance. At 0°C, a Li-ion battery may only be able to deliver $80\%$ to $90\%$ of its rated capacity, and its ability to accept charge (regenerative braking) is severely curtailed to prevent lithium plating.
	\item \textbf{High Temperature Effects}: Operating above 35-40°C accelerates the chemical side reactions that drive aging. While capacity might initially increase slightly due to lower resistance, the long-term penalty is severe lifespan reduction.
\end{itemize}

Tugboat battery rooms are invariably climate-controlled (Liquid Cooling + HVAC) to maintain an optimal window of 20°C–25°C. 

A conservative Temperature Correction Factor of $1.05$ to $1.10$ ($5-10\%$ oversizing) is applied. This ensures that the rated energy is available even if the ambient conditions in the battery room deviate from the ISO standard day.

\subsubsection{e) Capacity Rating Factor and Peukert Efficiency ($k_{c-rate}$)}

Peukert's Law ($C_p = I^k t$) describes how the available capacity of a battery decreases as the discharge rate increases. For Lithium-ion batteries, the effect is minimal, with $k$ values typically between 1.01 and 1.05.

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Low Power Applications}: For hotel loads ($< 0.5C$), the factor is negligible ($\sim 1.0$).
	\item \textbf{High Power Applications}: For propulsion boost ($> 2C$), a derating factor is prudent.
\end{itemize}

To calculate this factor, an initial ${C}_{rate}$ can be made based on the required energy ${E}_{load}$ and the service power ${P}_{service}$ using the following formula \cite{MAN}:

\begin{equation}
{C}_{rate}[{h}^{-1}]=\frac{{E}_{load}[kWh]}{{P}_{service}[kWh]}
\end{equation}

This provides an approximation of the ${C}_{rate}$ to determine the value of the $k_{c-rate}$ factor.
\subsection{Battery Sizing}

Based on the factors described previously, the calculations used to obtain the total installed energy ${E}_{\text{installed}}$ are presented:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Total Load}: This corresponds to adding the energy from the load profile ${E}_{prof}$ to the energy used for hotel loads ${E}_{hotel}$ $${E}_{load}={E}_{prof}+{E}_{hotel}$$
	\item \textbf{C-rate}: Initial calculation of the ${C}_{rate}$ to determine the associated factor $k_{c-rate}$ 
\begin{align*}
{C}_{rate}=\frac{{E}_{load}}{{P}_{service}}<0 & \Rightarrow k_{c-rate} = 1\\
{C}_{rate}=\frac{{E}_{load}}{{P}_{service}}\geq 0 & \Rightarrow k_{c-rate} >1
\end{align*}
	
	\item \textbf{System Efficiency}: To supply power to the mechanical shafts, the battery must generate $${E}_{batt{\_}out}={E}_{load}/{\eta}_{sys}$$
	\item \textbf{Max DOD}: Ensure that the battery is only used to the appropriate depth of discharge relative to its initial nominal capacity (beginning of life, BOL).  To do this, calculate $${E}_{BOL}={E}_{batt{\_}out}/{DOD}_{max}$$
	\item \textbf{Aging Factor}: To ensure that the battery bank has a long service life, the aging factor must be taken into account  $${E}_{EOL}={E}_{BOL}/{k}_{aging}$$
	\item \textbf{Apply Temperature/Rating Factors}: Consider the variation in temperature and battery discharge rate through $${E}_{installed}={E}_{EOL}\cdot{k}_{temp}\cdot{k}_{c-rate}$$
\end{enumerate}

The following equation is presented to obtain the total installed capacity based on the factors explained above.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:Energy_Batt}
	{E}_{installed}= \frac{{E}_{load}\cdot{k}_{temp}\cdot{k}_{c-rate}}{{DOD}_{max}\cdot{k}_{aging}\cdot{\eta}_{sys}}
\end{equation}

\section{Nominal Battery Voltage}

The system voltage should be determined by the cost, desired generator and propulsion motor drive voltage, converter design, load requirements, cable and bus-bar rating, and the fault energy \cite{Xu2022}. Generally, low-voltage dc (LVdc) systems are lower than 1 kV, with power system capacity in the range of 1–20MW, while medium-voltage dc (MVdc) systems are higher than 1 kV, with 20–100 MW.


\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{750V DC}: This range is common in earlier hybrid designs, smaller tugboats, or retrofits where space for series-connected modules might be limited \cite{Valence}. It balances safety requirements with the need for power density but becoming less favorable for tugs with MW-scale power requirements due to the heavy cabling required.
	\item \textbf{1000V DC}: This is the emerging standard for high-power vessels. Higher voltage reduces the current required for the same power ($P=VI$) and copper losses ($P_{loss} = I^2R$).
\end{itemize}

The battery bank is constructed of modules in series to achieve the voltage.

\begin{equation}
	N_{series} = V_{bus\_req} / V_{cell\_nom}
\end{equation}

For the battery bank in both options, a nominal voltage of $V_{bus\_req}=750$[V] will be proposed.



\section{Battery technologies applicable to tugboats}

Although several battery technologies for different vessels were mentioned in section \ref{sec:Roles}, in the field of hybrid and electric tugboats, three main lithium-ion chemical compositions dominate this sector \cite{BattForum}:

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Nickel Manganese Cobalt (NMC)},
\item \textbf{Lithium Iron Phosphate (LFP)},
\item \textbf{Lithium Titanate Oxide (LTO)}.

\end{itemize}


\subsection{Nickel Manganese Cobalt (NMC)}

NMC technology (LiNiMnCoO\textsubscript{2}) is preferred for
electric vehicles and within the
maritime industry as its life cycle is
long while the energy density is
satisfying \cite{MAN}.

NMC has historically been the chemistry of choice for first-generation hybrid vessels due to its high specific energy. 

Main features on NMC include the following:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Energy Density}: NMC cells offer between 150 and 220 Wh/kg, which translates into lower densities after taking into account the BMS, cooling, and rack \cite{UFine}.
	\item \textbf{C-rate}: Standard marine NMC modules (e.g., Corvus Orca Energy) support high discharge rates, typically 3C continuous \cite{Corvus}, ideal for the high power density demands of a tugboat.
	\item \textbf{Cycle Life}: NMC typically offers between 1000 and 1500 cycles \cite{evlithium}, which may be sufficient for operating modes where the battery is used for peak shaving rather than performing full cycles every day.
	\item \textbf{Cost}: 
Cell prices range from $\text{US }\$120-\text{US }\$150/\text{kWh}$ \cite{UFine}. The main drawback is thermal stability; NMC has a lower thermal runaway temperature, requiring robust active cooling systems that could increase its cost \cite{Sunreef}. 	
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Lithium Iron Phosphate (LFP)}
LFP (LiFePO\textsubscript{4}) is rapidly gaining market share in the maritime sector, driven by safety and longevity.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Energy Density}: The cell density is 90-160 Wh/kg \cite{evlithium}. Tugboats require considerable ballast to achieve the draft necessary for propeller immersion. LFP batteries could replace lead or iron ballast, serving a dual function as a “\textbf{stability weight”} \cite{UFine}, representing around $30\%$ of the total ballast, which could reach approximately $55 [{m}^{3}]$ \cite{cotecmar}.
	\item \textbf{Safety}: The simple chemistry of LFP batteries (no nickel or cobalt) makes them safe and robust. These batteries are used in applications where safety and long life are more important than compact size \cite{Bonnen}.
	\item  \textbf{Cycle Life}: The LFP offers between 2,000 and more than 5,000 cycles, even with a high discharge depth \cite{evlithium}. Some sources suggest that it lasts between 2 and 3 times longer than the NMC \cite{UFine}, making it ideal for applications that require longer life and reliability.
	
	\item \textbf{Cost}: The LFP has an estimated cell cost of between $\text{US }\$80-\text{US }\$100/\text{kWh}$, making it $30\%$ cheaper than the NMC option in 2026 \cite{UFine}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Lithium Titanate Oxide (LTO)}

Lithium titanate batteries (Li\textsubscript{4}Ti\textsubscript{5}O\textsubscript{12}) are the only ones with a unique anode surface made from lithium and titanium oxides. These batteries for electric boats, also known as LTO batteries, operate in extreme weather conditions, offering greater safety \cite{Sunreef}.

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Performance}: One of the main advantages of LTO is its high C-rate capacity (i.e., high maximum power) \cite{Echandia}. It offers exceptional service life (more than 10,000 cycles) \cite{evlithium}.
	\item  \textbf{Drawbacks}: LTO has the lowest energy density (60–120 Wh/kg) and the highest cost \cite{evlithium}.
	\item \textbf{Cycle Life}: LTO batteries can operate safely for 25,000 cycles, achieving a service life of more than 20 years \cite{Echandia}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Calculation of essential electrical parameters}

\textbf{The number of battery cells connected in series} $\text{N}_{sc} [-]$ in a string is calculated by dividing the nominal battery pack voltage $\text{V}_{bp} [\text{V}]$ to the voltage of each battery cell $\text{V}_{bc} [\text{V}]$. The number of strings must be an integer. Therefore, the result of the calculation is rounded to the higher integer.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ncs}
\text{N}_{sc} = \frac{\text{V}_{bp}}{\text{V}_{bc}}
\end{equation}

\textbf{The energy content of a string} $\text{E}_{bs} [\text{Wh}]$ is equal with the product between the number of battery cells connected in series $\text{N}_{sc} [-]$ and the energy of a battery cell $\text{E}_{bc} [\text{Wh}]$.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ebs}
\text{E}_{bs} = \text{N}_{sc} \cdot \text{E}_{bc}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Total number of strings of the battery pack} $\text{N}_{sb} [-]$ is calculated by dividing the battery pack total energy $\text{E}_{bp} [\text{Wh}]$ to the energy content of a string $\text{E}_{bs} [\text{Wh}]$. The number of strings must be an integer. Therefore, the result of the calculation is rounded to the higher integer.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:Nsb}
\text{N}_{sb} = \frac{\text{E}_{bp}}{\text{E}_{bs}}
\end{equation}

%It is now possible to recalculate the battery pack total energy ${E}_{bp} [Wh]$ as the product between number of strings ${N}_{sb} [-]$ and the energy content of each string ${E}_{bs} [Wh]$.

\textbf{The battery pack capacity} $\text{C}_{bp} [\text{Ah}]$ is calculated as the product between the number of strings $\text{N}_{sb} [-]$ and the capacity of the battery $\text{C}_{bc} [\text{Ah}]$.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:Cbp}
\text{C}_{bp} = \text{N}_{sb} \cdot\text{C}_{bc}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Total number of cells of the battery pack} $\text{N}_{bp} [-]$ is calculated as the product between the number of strings $\text{N}_{sb} [-]$ and the number of cells in a string $\text{N}_{sc} [-]$.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ncb}
\text{N}_{bp} = \text{N}_{sb} \cdot \text{N}_{sc}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Calculation of electrical performance parameters}

\textbf{The string continuous current} $\text{I}_{scc} [\text{A}]$ is the product between the continuous C-rate of the battery cell $\text{C-rate}_{bcc} [\text{h}^{-1}]$ and the battery cell capacity $\text{C}_{bc} [\text{Ah}]$.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:Iscc}
{I}_{scc} = \text{C-rate}_{bcc} \cdot \text{C}_{bc}
\end{equation}

\textbf{The battery pack continuous current} $\text{I}_{bpc} [\text{A}]$ is the product between the string continuous current $\text{I}_{scc} [\text{A}]$ and the number of strings of the battery pack $\text{N}_{sb} [-]$.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ibpc}
\text{I}_{bpc} = \text{I}_{scc} \cdot \text{N}_{sb}
\end{equation}


\textbf{The battery pack continuous power} $\text{P}_{bpc} [\text{W}]$ is the product between battery pack continuous current $\text{I}_{bpc} [\text{A}]$ and the battery pack voltage $\text{V}_{bp} [\text{V}]$.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:Pbpc}
\text{P}_{bpc} = \text{I}_{bpc} \cdot \text{V}_{bp}
\end{equation}

\textbf{The string peak current} $\text{I}_{spc} [\text{A}]$ is the product between the peak C-rate of the battery cell $\text{C-rate}_{bpc} [\text{h}^{-1}]$ and the battery cell capacity $\text{C}_{bc} [\text{Ah}]$.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ispc}
\text{I}_{spc} = \text{C-rate}_{bpc} \cdot \text{C}_{bc}
\end{equation}

\textbf{The battery pack peak current} $\text{I}_{bpp} [\text{A}]$ is the product between the string peak current $\text{I}_{spc} [\text{A}]$ and the number of strings of the battery pack $\text{N}_{sb} [-]$.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ibpp}
\text{I}_{bpp} = \text{I}_{spc} \cdot \text{N}_{sb}
\end{equation}

\textbf{The battery pack peak power} $\text{P}_{bpp} [\text{W}]$ is the product between battery pack peak current $\text{I}_{bpp} [\text{A}]$ and the battery pack voltage $\text{V}_{bp} [\text{V}]$.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:Pbpp}
\text{P}_{bpp} = \text{I}_{bpp} \cdot \text{V}_{bp}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Calculation of mechanical parameters}

\textbf{The battery pack mass} (cells only) $\text{m}_{bp} [\text{kg}]$ is the product between the total number of cells $\text{N}_{bp} [-]$ and the mass of each battery cell $\text{m}_{bc} [\text{kg}]$.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mbp}
\text{m}_{bp} = \text{N}_{bp} \cdot \text{m}_{bc}
\end{equation}

\textbf{The volume of the battery pack} (cells only) $\text{vol}_{bp} [\text{m}^{3}]$ is the product between the total number of cells $\text{N}_{bp} [-]$ and the volume of each battery cell $\text{vol}_{bc} [\text{m}^{3}]$. This volume is only used to estimate the final volume of the battery pack, since it does not take into account the auxiliary components/systems of the battery \cite{x-engineer}.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:volpc}
\text{vol}_{bp} = \text{N}_{bp} \cdot \text{vol}_{bc}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Mechanical design considerations}

The mechanical design is focused on the calculation of the total weight and volume of the battery bank \cite{LSZ}.

As mentioned in section \ref{sec:VW}, \textbf{gross tonnage (GT)} is a measure of a ship's total internal volume, while \textbf{net tonnage (NT)} is a ship's gross tonnage minus the space occupied by crew quarters, machinery, navigation equipment, engine rooms, and fuel storage. Therefore, net tonnage represents the space available for passenger, accommodation and cargo storage.

Additionally, \textbf{deadweight tonnage (DWT)} measures how much weight a vessel can safely carry, including the combined weight of cargo, fuel, fresh water, ballast water, provisions, passengers, and crew.

\section{Economic Analysis}

To analyze the economic viability of a hybrid tugboat, it is necessary to first understand the higher initial capital expenditures (CAPEX) and operating expenses (OPEX).

In addition, two additional factors related to emissions taxes and government subsidies are taken into account to analyze how this affects economic indicators and how sensitive they are: 



\begin{itemize}
	\item A CO\textsubscript{2} emissions tax of $\$50/{\text{t}}_{\text{CO\textsubscript{2}}}$.
	\item A government subsidy of $10\%$ of CAPEX.
\end{itemize}



\subsection{Capital Expenditure (CAPEX)}

One of the major problems facing the maritime transport sector with regard to the advancement of hybrid propulsion is how to achieve a fully satisfactory implementation of a cost-effective hybrid propulsion plant \cite{MAN2}. 
The main challenge is CAPEX, i.e., keeping the capital expenditure of hybrid equipment in line with that of conventional propulsion plants.

The cost of marine battery systems is significantly higher than automotive packs due to the "marine tax"—the cost of ruggedization, certification, and low-volume manufacturing.\\

BESS is a mature technology and there are already hybrid tugboats that have been deployed \cite{CaGov}. A BESS contains Li-ion batteries, a battery management system, ventilation/cooling, and a dedicated fire extinguishing system. The BESS must be installed in a dedicated compartment with ventilation. However, Li-ion batteries have a very low energy density, and large battery installations will entail space and weight costs on the vessel\\ 
Fire safety is also a concern, as battery fires can be difficult to extinguish. The lifetime of the BESS is also limited by the number of charge/discharge cycles. The typical BESS lifetime is 8,000 to 12,000 cycles.\\

It is important to note that this is the estimated cost for the first tugboat \cite{CaGov}. This distinction is important because the first hybrid tugboat is considered a novel design, which requires additional engineering and design work hours.Therefore, $3.5\%$ of the gross cost will be considered, which is a similar engineering cost in proportion to that seen in other projects \cite{CaGov}.

The following are the main elements that are considered for the hybridization and retrofitting of a conventional tugboat.

\subsubsection{a) Battery Bank NMC}

Their life cycles are around 1,000–2,500 cycles. In a heavy-duty tug application with frequent peaks, NMC packs may degrade faster, necessitating a costly replacement mid-lifecycle (Year 7–10), which severely impacts the NPV \cite{ankersolix}.

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{System Price (2024/2025)} \cite{ritarpower}: $\text{US}\$600-\text{US}\$1000$ per kWh.	
\end{itemize}

The higher cost is due to the use of expensive raw materials such as cobalt and the more complex manufacturing processes required to achieve the high energy density and performance characteristics \cite{ritarpower}. For example, in electric vehicles where space and weight are critical factors, NMC batteries are often preferred despite the higher cost, as they can provide longer driving ranges without adding excessive bulk to the vehicle.

\subsubsection{b) Battery Bank LFP}

While LFP has a lower energy density (90 to 160 Wh/kg) than NMC, it offers a superior cycle life, typically achieving 2,000 to 6,000+ cycles. In many maritime applications, such as cargo vessels or mid-speed ferries, the slightly higher weight of LFP is an acceptable trade-off for the increased safety and the ability to avoid replacement for 10 years or more \cite{evlithium}. 
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{System Price (2024/2025)} \cite{ritarpower}: $\text{US}\$300-\text{US}\$600$ per kWh.
\end{itemize}

The lower cost can be attributed to the use of more abundant and less expensive raw materials, as well as a simpler manufacturing process \cite{ritarpower}. Although they have a slightly lower energy density compared to NMC batteries, they are a popular choice for applications where safety and long-term reliability are of utmost importance, such as in home energy storage systems and some electric vehicles where cost is a significant consideration.

\subsubsection{c) Battery Bank LTO}

LTO systems can withstand 10,000 to 15,000+ cycles and are capable of ultra-fast charging, often reaching full capacity in 10 to 30 minutes \cite{evlithium}.

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{System Price (2024/2025)} \cite{ritarpower}: $\text{US}\$800-\text{US}\$1,200$ per kWh.
\end{itemize}

The higher cost is mainly due to the production process of lithium titanate, which is more complex and requires specialized equipment. Additionally, the raw materials used in LTO batteries can be costly \cite{ritarpower}. Despite their high cost, LTO batteries are finding applications in areas where rapid charging and high power output are crucial, such as in some electric buses and high-performance electric vehicles.\\

\subsubsection{d) Power Electronics}

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{DC/DC Converters}: The DC/DC converters convert and regulate the power supplied from the lithium ion batteries to the voltage requirement of the DC bus \cite{ProfMar}. 
	\begin{itemize}
		\item  \textbf{System Price} \cite{Akbarzadeh} \cite{TestEq}: $\text{US}\$100-\text{US}\$400$ per kW.
	\end{itemize}	
	
	\item \textbf{Inverters}: Bidirectional AFE that regulates the voltage from a battery-linked DC/DC converter to an AC propulsion bus, with marine-grade and liquid-cooled drives \cite{Wartsila3}. 
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{System Price}: $\text{US}\$250-\text{US}\$450$ per kW.
	\end{itemize}	
	\item \textbf{Variable Frequency Drives}: AC drives function as the primary interface between the electrical distribution system—whether it be a traditional AC grid or a modern common DC bus—and the three-phase motors that provide mechanical thrust \cite{Radica}. 
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{System Price}: $\text{US}\$120-\text{US}\$250$ per kW.
	\end{itemize}	
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{e) Main genset and Electric motors}

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Main Genset}: The price ranges for diesel generators with a power output of 2000 kW (2 MW) vary significantly based on the manufacturer (Tier 1 vs. budget brands), application (Marine vs. Industrial), and condition (New vs. Used).
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{System Price}: $\text{US}\$75-\text{US}\$750$ per kW.
	\end{itemize}	
	\item \textbf{Electric Motors:} The price for a 1,000 kW (1 MW) squirrel cage induction motor suitable for hybrid tugboat propulsion varies significantly depending on the manufacturer (ABB, Siemens (SIMOTICS M), WEG, Danfoss Editron), cooling method, and classification status. 
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{System Price}: $\text{US}\$90-\text{US}\$250$ per kW.
	\end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{f) Integration and Safety systems}

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Integration/cabling}: It is considered to be around $20\%$ of the cost of equipment.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{System Price}: $\text{US}\$100,000-\text{US}\$175,0000$.
	\end{itemize}	
	\item \textbf{Fire/Safety Systems:} Battery rooms must be A-60 fire-rated, gas-tight, and equipped with explosion relief vents and specialized ventilation to handle potential off-gassing, adding structural steel and HVAC costs \cite{Norwegian}. 
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{System Price}: $\text{US}\$50,000-\text{US}\$80,000$.
	\end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Operational Expenditure (OPEX)}

OPEX savings are derived primarily from fuel reduction during "loitering" (low load) and maintenance intervals extended by reduced engine runtime \cite{MAN2}. 


Thus, the following variables are considered in operating expenses.

\subsubsection{a) Fuel Consumption Savings}

A conventional tugboat consumes approximately several thousand liters of fuel just idling during its daily workday. A hybrid tugboat in electric mode consumes 0 liters in that mode. The batteries allow the load on the main engines to be balanced, thereby improving the specific fuel consumption (BSFC).

\subsubsection{b) Maintenance Engine Reduction}

Hybrid tugs can reduce main engine operating hours by up to $60\%$ per year by using batteries for transit and standby mode. This extends the overhaul intervals for propulsion engines, reducing annualized maintenance costs. However, these savings in propulsion engine maintenance are partially offset by the need to budget for battery replacement, so an “amortization fund” must be established in the financial model to cover battery pack replacement \cite{Wartsila2}. For NMC/LFP, replacement will be considered after 15 years, while for LTO, a useful life of 30 years will be considered, exceeding the project period, so no replacement cost will be amortized for this technology.

\subsubsection{c) Total Costs}

\subsection{Financial Performance}		


Based on the information described above, the viability of the project can be determined using economic indicators such as net present value (NPV), internal rate of return (IRR), and finally return on investment (ROI).



\subsubsection{a) Net Present Value (NPV)}

The following equation will be used to calculate the NPV.

\begin{equation}
\text{NPV} = \sum_{t=1}^{\text{LFS}} \frac{\text{Prf}}{(1+\text{ds})^t} - \text{Inv}.
\end{equation}

Where:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{LFS}: Lifespan of the project (years).
	\item \textbf{Prf}: Annual profit.
	\item \textbf{ds}: Discount rate.
	\item \textbf{Inv}: Initial Investment.
\end{itemize}

For all scenarios, a \textbf{discount rate} of $\bf 8\%$ and a useful life of 10 and 20 years for the hybrid tugboat will be proposed. In this way, the positive NPV will demonstrate that the project will generate a return on investment.

In addition, it is proposed that the batteries used will have a lifespan of 3,000 to 5,000 cycles during the lifetime of this project.

\subsubsection{b) Internal Rate of Return (IRR)}

IRR is an indicator of a project's profitability that is used to decide whether to accept or reject an investment project \cite{MinHac}. To do this, the IRR is compared to a minimum rate or discount rate. If the project's rate of return—expressed by the IRR—exceeds the discount rate, it is accepted; otherwise, it is rejected.
The IRR can be calculated using the following equation:

\begin{equation}
0 = \sum_{t=1}^{\text{LFS}} \frac{\text{Prf}}{(1+\text{IRR})^t} - \text{Inv}.
\end{equation}

Where:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{LFS}: Lifespan of the project (years).
	\item \textbf{Prf}: Annual profit.
	\item \textbf{Inv}: Initial Investment.
	\item \textbf{IRR}: Internal rate of return.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{c) Return on Investment (ROI)/ Payback Period}

Return on investment (ROI) is a measure of profitability. It indicates how much profit or loss a company makes from an investment in relation to its cost. Measuring return on investment helps identify which projects truly drive profitability, optimize resources, and strengthen the company's competitiveness.

The formula is as follows:

\begin{equation}
\text{ROI} = \frac{\text{G} - \text{Inv}}{\text{Inv}}\cdot 100
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{G}: Annual gain from investment.
	\item \textbf{Inv}: Initial Investment.
\end{itemize}

To calculate the period in which the investment is recouped and profits are generated ${\text{P}}_{\text{ROI}}$, the following formula is used:

\begin{equation}
{\text{P}}_{\text{ROI}} = \frac{\text{Inv}}{\text{G}}
\end{equation}

\section{Chapter Summary}

This chapter has the focus on the preliminary design, technical evaluation, and economic viability of an Energy Storage System (ESS) for a hybrid tugboat. The chapter outlines the methodology for sizing battery banks and compares three primary lithium-ion technologies: NMC, LFP, and LTO.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Sizing Methodology and Requirements}: The design process begins by calculating the required energy capacity based on the tugboat's operational load profile ($E_{load}$), which includes both propulsion and "hotel" (auxiliary) loads. To determine the final installed capacity ($E_{installed}$), it was applied several correction factors.
\item \textbf{Technology Comparison}: It was compared Nickel Manganese Cobalt (NMC), Lithium Iron Phosphate (LFP), and Lithium Titanate Oxide (LTO), , based on market options. The comparison of alternatives was carried out in a particularly technical manner. 
\item \textbf{Economic and Financial Results}: The economic analysis considers the Capital Expenditure (CAPEX) of retrofitting a tugboat and the Operational Expenditure (OPEX) savings from reduced fuel consumption.
\end{enumerate}

The conclusion of the design analysis is that LFP technology is the most viable solution for hybrid tugboats, as it balances lower initial cost with a long service life, enabling a faster return on investment compared to NMC or LTO.
